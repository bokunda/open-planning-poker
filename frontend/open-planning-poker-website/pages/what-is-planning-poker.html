What is planning poker?

Planning poker is a way for an agile team to estimate work together. Everyone picks a card in secret, the cards are revealed at the same moment, and the people with the highest and lowest cards explain their reasoning. Then the team votes again.

How a round works

  1. Read the item. The product owner or host reads the user story or ticket and answers questions.
  2. Everyone picks a card in secret. Each card is a size on the team's scale, for example 1, 2, 3, 5, 8 or 13.
  3. Reveal together. All cards turn over at the same moment, so nobody is influenced by the first number they hear.
  4. Talk about the outliers. If the votes are far apart, the people with the lowest and highest cards explain what they see that others don't.
  5. Vote again until the votes are close, then record the estimate and move on.

An example round

A team of five estimates "Export results to CSV". The cards come up as 3, 5, 5, 5 and 13. Stefan picked 3 because the export library is already in the project. Marko picked 13 because the CSV has to include every vote for every ticket, which needs a new query. After two minutes the team agrees the query is real work, votes again, and gets 5, 5, 5, 8, 5. They record 5.

The value of the round was not the number. It was finding out about the missing query before the sprint started.

Why it works

  • No anchoring. When one person says a number first, the rest drift toward it. Secret votes remove that effect.
  • Everyone speaks. Quiet team members and new joiners put a card on the table like everyone else.
  • Disagreement is useful. A spread of votes shows that people understand the work differently, which is exactly what a team needs to know before it starts.
  • Relative sizes are easier than hours. People are better at saying "this is about twice that one" than predicting hours.

Common mistakes

  • Averaging without talking. If the votes are 2 and 13, the average hides the real question. Ask the outliers first.
  • Turning points into hours. Story points measure size relative to other items, not time. Mixing the two leads to pressure to "be accurate".
  • Estimating items that are too big. If the team keeps voting 40 or 100, split the item.
  • Letting the most senior person go first. That brings anchoring back. Everyone reveals at once.

Planning poker online

Remote and hybrid teams cannot hold up physical cards, so they use an online tool. Open Planning Poker is free and open source: create a room, share the link or QR code, and everyone votes from a laptop or phone. Cards stay hidden until the host reveals them, and the results show the average, the median and who should explain. Frequently asked questions

Who invented planning poker?

James Grenning described planning poker in 2002. Mike Cohn made it widely known in his 2005 book Agile Estimating and Planning.

Is planning poker only for Scrum?

No. Any team that estimates work items can use it: Scrum, Kanban teams that size their backlog, or a project team planning a release.

What do the numbers mean?

They are relative sizes, usually story points. A 5 is roughly twice the effort of a 2 or 3. They are not hours.

How long should a round take?

One to three minutes per item is typical. If a discussion runs much longer, the item is probably too big or unclear and should be split or researched first.
